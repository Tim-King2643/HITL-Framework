The Shock Wave Effect

How change propagates through an organization — and what happens when it hits multiple domains at once

Where This Started

The Working Collaboration & Co-Development Principles document's Shared Vision paragraph names the real question underneath this whole partnership: when AI, or any other force, materially changes the work, what happens to the role, the person performing it, the manager leading it, the team around it, and the accountability for what follows?

“Our working vision is to create an integrated architecture for Human + AI Work — one that connects the architecture of work with the human consequences of changing that work.”

That question has a shape worth naming directly: it widens outward, ring by ring, from the point of impact. That's the shock wave — and once you see the org that way, two things follow. First, a single role's transformation propagates through five distinct rings, not one. Second, when several parts of the organization transform at once, those individual waves don't stay separate.

The Blast Radius of a Single Role

RingWhat ChangesMeasured Today?
RoleThe work itself — which activities move along the working-relationship ramp, from Human-only toward Automation, and how far each is allowed to goYes — working-relationship classification: each activity's Current State and Progression Ceiling

ManagerThe manager who has to lead someone through the change, redistribute what's left, and absorb the disruption to their own span of controlPartial — reportsTo and span of control exist; the force of the impact does not
TeamThe team around the role, absorbing whatever gets redistributed once the role itself changesNo — not yet built
AccountabilityWho remains answerable for the decision or outcome, no matter how the underlying work changesYes — RACI Accountable, Governed Decision Points. Held fixed by design, not expected to move
Governed Decision Point (GDP): a specific activity where a role holds real decision-making authority, recorded with three things — who decides normally, who it escalates to, and the trigger condition that moves a decision up past them. It's the mechanism behind “accountability is held fixed” — proof that the accountable person for the real judgment call hasn't changed, even as the surrounding work does.

What's worth noticing: the two rings the framework already measures well — Role and Accountability — sit at the two ends of the wave, not in the middle. Accountability is deliberately built to hold its shape regardless of how hard the wave hits everything around it; that's the framework's own founding thesis (“AI can redistribute work. It cannot redistribute accountability.”) restated in blast-radius terms. The middle three rings — Person, Manager, Team — are where the actual human cost of the wave is absorbed, and it's exactly those rings where the framework is thinnest today.

When Multiple Domains Detonate at Once

A single role's transformation is a contained event — hard at the center, weaker with distance, absorbable. The real risk sharpens when AI transformation hits several functional domains at the same time, uncoordinated, without any sequencing between them.

Overlapping shock waves don't simply add together — where two waves cross, they interfere, and the combined force at that intersection can exceed either wave alone. The organizational parallel is precise: a manager, a shared-services team, or a role sitting at the intersection of two or three functions transforming simultaneously doesn't experience double the disruption. They experience the interference pattern — no recovery time between hits, no slack nearby to borrow from, and the people and trust at exactly those overlap points failing first, before the damage is even visible anywhere else.

From Metaphor to Mechanism: Cascade, Not Annihilation

“Total nuclear destruction” is the right image for how it feels from inside an uncoordinated, multi-domain change effort — but the more precise and more defensible failure mode is cascading failure, the same vocabulary systems engineering and financial-contagion analysis use for interconnected systems. An isolated failure is absorbable. A failure that hits multiple interconnected nodes at once, with nothing containing it, cascades: trust erodes organization-wide rather than locally, decision-making seizes up because accountability itself is overwhelmed, and the damage compounds faster than the organization can respond to any single piece of it.

That's a real and severe failure mode — and, importantly, a survivable and preventable one, which “total destruction” as a literal claim is not. The nuclear image works to make the stakes vivid; cascading failure is the mechanism to point to if the argument ever needs to hold up analytically rather than just land emotionally.

The Framework's Answer: Containment Through Sequencing

This isn't a hypothetical the framework has no response to. Governed Decision Points holding accountability fixed at every stage, and a phased, one-domain-at-a-time rollout rather than a simultaneous org-wide push, are together a containment strategy against exactly this failure mode — even though nothing built so far has been described in those terms. Sequencing keeps the shock waves from overlapping in the first place; governance keeps accountability from being one of the things that erodes if they do.

A Second Path: Work Product

The five rings trace the wave from a role out through people. Work product carries it a second way. An activity's output — a record, a review, a report — is read by other processes, and each of those has its own Accountable role. When AI produces or drafts that output, every downstream Accountable role inherits a job: confirming it can be trusted before acting on it. GiGi named this the verification tax (HR Couch, Sept 23, 2026): “If AI saves someone ten hours but another employee spends six hours checking the work… we did not eliminate ten hours. We moved the work somewhere else.”

The framework can already trace where that work moves. The Data View records which process creates each work product and which processes read it; RACI names who is Accountable at each step; the working-relationship classification shows how much of the producing work AI does. Tracing the Performance Review through the Sandbox 7.0 data:

So an AI-drafted review does not stop at the review: it feeds promotion, pay, labor relations, and retention decisions. The verification tax lands on whoever is Accountable at each of those four points. The HRBP Manager pays it twice: once at the source, and again when their own promotion and labor decisions rely on it.

Running the same trace across every work product in Sandbox 7.0 shows where the verification load concentrates. Eleven work products have an AI-involved producer and at least one downstream reader:

The role that already holds the most Accountable judgment calls in 7.0 — grievances, promotions, labor relations — is also where the most AI-produced work lands for checking. For each work product, four things are worth measuring:

  • Fan-out: how many downstream processes read it. The Employee Master Record is written by an Automation-level process and read by 14.
  • Consequence of what it feeds: a review feeding a promotion decision matters more than one feeding a newsletter.
  • The verification point: the first place an AI-produced output meets a Judgment or Oversight step, or an Accountable role — where someone has to answer where it came from, whether it was verified, and who stands behind the decision.
  • Verification load: the roles that sit at many verification points at once.

These counts are modeled from Sandbox 7.0, not observed. “AI-involved” means at least one activity in the producing process sits at Augmentation or further along the ramp, and the Data View records CRUD per process for 22 of 37 processes, so the trace shows which process produces a work product but not yet which activity inside it.

Open Questions

Manager and Team as measured rings. Both rings currently have structural data (reportsTo, span of control) but no measure of force or load — this is the same open ground as the framework's existing Trust and load/sustainability gaps, just named from a different direction.

Verification load as the first measure of the middle rings. Counting AI-touched work products per Accountable role is a computed, repeatable signal of where load lands — the first real candidate for measuring the Manager and Team rings. It still counts exposure, not hours; turning it into effort would need observed data.

Whether interference is measurable, not just descriptive. Could the framework ever flag a real overlap — a manager or team sitting at the intersection of two simultaneously-transforming domains — before it happens, rather than only naming the concept after the fact?

Scope. Right now this is written from PCF 7.0 (HR) outward. Whether it stays a narrative frame or becomes something closer to a formal cross-domain lens is undecided.
