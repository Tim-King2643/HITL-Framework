Working-Relationship Progression

What it does, what it replaces, and what it's proven on — a plain-language companion to the full methodology document
Documentation Dashboard Work in Progress
This needs Tim and GiGi's approval before it becomes official

The full methodology (Working-Relationship Progression — A Design Methodology) has been live on the site as a draft since September, and it's the thing the sandbox is actually built on — but it was never added to the WIP catalog or wired to voting, so there's no record of anyone formally signing off on it. This page is two things at once: a plain-language rewrite for anyone who doesn't need the full technical detail, and the vehicle for bringing the underlying methodology into the same approval process every other framework decision goes through.

Related Requirements — Requirements Register
  • GR-044 — Sandbox Environment (Implemented — this methodology is the sandbox's data model)

Short version: the framework used to guess how much of an activity a human still needed to do based on what kind of activity it was. That guess was often wrong. This replaces the guess with a direct classification of what's actually happening, activity by activity — and adds a gate that has to clear before any activity is allowed to automate further.

The Problem This Solves

The old model looked at an activity's type — Decision, Knowledge, Document, Transaction, or Exception — and used that type to predict a single number for how much of it still needed a human. The trouble: two activities of completely different types kept landing on the exact same real-world answer (a human checking each instance before it goes through), while two activities that scored almost identically on that number turned out to involve completely different kinds of work — one mostly judgment calls and coaching, the other mostly compliance and exception-handling. The type was a poor stand-in for the thing that actually matters: what is the human doing, and why does it have to be a human who does it.

What It Does

1. Classifies every activity on one clear ramp, not a type-based guess

Every activity gets placed directly on a six-step scale, based on what's actually happening today — not inferred from its category:

Human-only
→
Judgment
→
Oversight
→
Augmentation
→
Agent-delegation
→
Automation

An activity moves one step at a time — it can't skip a step on the way to more automation. The five work-pattern colors (Decision/Knowledge/Document/Transaction/Exception) keep doing their own separate job elsewhere on the site; this is a different, additional lens, not a replacement for them.

2. Tracks two numbers per activity instead of one five-point score

  • Current State — where the activity actually sits today.
  • Progression Ceiling — the furthest this activity can ever go, and the reason it stops there (sometimes that reason is a standing duty that can't be delegated at all; sometimes it's simply that full automation is the natural end point and there's nowhere further to go).

Current State and Progression Ceiling aren't always the same value — an activity can still have real runway ahead of it, or it can already be sitting at its ceiling with nowhere further to go.

3. Puts detail where it belongs, and rolls up where it doesn't

An activity's classification lives at the activity level, because that's genuinely where the evidence is. But a manager's capacity to absorb change, or a team's readiness for it, isn't a property of any single activity — those get measured as a rollup of everything changing underneath them, not reclassified activity-by-activity themselves.

4. Requires three independent checks before an activity is allowed to move forward

No activity advances toward its Progression Ceiling on the strength of one score. Three separate signals all have to clear first — real evidence that the technology performs reliably on this specific work, real evidence the process around it can support the change, and real evidence the people affected are actually ready for it. One good number doesn't substitute for the other two.

5. Traces how a change ripples outward, with the same questions at every stage

When one activity changes, the effects don't stop there — they move outward to the role, the person doing it, their manager, their team, and ultimately who's accountable for the outcome. The same six questions get asked at each stage: what changed, who absorbs it, where did the work go, what new risk appeared, what new capability does someone need, and who owns the outcome now. One consistent question set instead of a different instrument for every stage.

Proven So Far

Tested against three roles spanning the full range of the organization, on purpose — not three similar roles that would all tell the same story.

HRBP Manager
Managerial tier · first pilot

Where the method was built and first tested. Surfaced a real correction: the reporting line the site had on record didn't match what the evidence actually showed.

Recruiter
Operational tier · highest volume

The busiest, most repetitive end of the range. Its highest-stakes single decision — selecting or rejecting a candidate — held up as the most human-critical activity in the whole pilot.

CHRO
Executive tier · narrowest scope

The opposite end — one strategic area instead of hundreds of transactions. Here the method confirmed what everyone already expected (this work is human-heavy) rather than overturning it, which is itself a useful result: the method doesn't just find surprises, it also correctly recognizes when there isn't one.

The method held up across all three without needing to be changed for each one — but it didn't produce the same kind of finding every time, and that's expected, not a weakness.

What's Still Open

How to measure the effort involved, not just how much is left

Right now the model can say a human is still doing 20% of an activity, but it can't yet say whether that remaining 20% is easy or genuinely demanding. That distinction isn't built yet.

Whether people actually have what they need to exercise real judgment

Authority, information, confidence, and the psychological safety to challenge a wrong AI recommendation — these matter, and there's an early idea for measuring them, but no working instrument yet.

Roles with no formal decision rights in the record

Some roles do real, valuable relationship and coaching work that a formal accountability record structurally can't see. Flagged honestly as a blind spot, not solved here.

A couple of edge cases from the CHRO pilot

What "the group this role can be compared against" means when a role sits at the very top with no peer to compare against, and what "who absorbs the change" means when there's no manager above to absorb it.

What the technology-readiness evidence should actually consist of, at scale

Still an open design question, not yet answered.
